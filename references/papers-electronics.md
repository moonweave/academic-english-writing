# Electronics: six patterns from three papers

Editorial analyses, inspected 2026-10-23. Full text accessible; named passages closely inspected. Do not inherit a paper's claims or every phrase with its structure.

## E1 — Pazos et al. (2025)

Source: Sebastian Pazos et al., “Synaptic and neural behaviours in a standard silicon transistor,” Nature 640, 69–76. [DOI and publisher full text](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-025-08742-4).

**E1a: Keep the condition inside the capability claim.** Location: abstract contribution sentences and “Punch-through impact ionization in MOSFETs,” opening comparison. Conditional clauses connect behavior to the bias and bulk configuration. Use for regime-specific claims; deleting the condition changes the claimed capability.

**E1b: Distinguish a component from an added control structure.** Location: Main final paragraph and opening results, two-transistor configuration. Concrete component names precede a statement of the added tuning function. Relative clauses attach function to the right component. Use when describing an actual implementation; one device's response does not establish a complete circuit's energy advantage. Manufacturing and reliability claims require their own evidence.

## E2 — Indiveri et al. (2011)

Source: Giacomo Indiveri et al., “Neuromorphic Silicon Neuron Circuits,” Frontiers in Neuroscience. [DOI/full text](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnins.2011.00073/full). Genre: review.

**E2a: Define function before implementation.** Location: Section 2 opening; Section 3.1 first circuit description. Functional blocks are introduced before circuit components. Parallel descriptions clarify responsibilities; implementation detail follows the established referent. Use when the abstraction and implementation match. Do not copy review-style breadth into a short Results paragraph.

**E2b: Compare along named design axes.** Location: Section 5, opening and discussion surrounding Table 2. The text compares precision, power, area, and application requirements instead of declaring one design universally superior. Contrastive clauses preserve trade-offs. Use with comparable evidence; qualify predictions and avoid borrowing expansive closing rhetoric.

## E3 — Shannon (1948)

Source: C. E. Shannon, “A Mathematical Theory of Communication,” Bell System Technical Journal 27, 379–423 and 623–656. [Part I DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/j.1538-7305.1948.tb01338.x); [corrected reprint](https://people.math.harvard.edu/~ctm/home/text/others/shannon/entropy/entropy.pdf). Analysis concerns the corrected reprint.

**E3a: Define the engineering problem and its scope.** Location: Introduction, reprint pp. 1–2. A precise problem statement precedes exclusions and component definitions. Definitional present tense and parallel relative clauses give terms stable meanings. Use for theory and architecture introductions; historical exclusions belong to that model, not all engineering problems.

**E3b: Explain the choice of a mathematical representation.** Location: reprint p. 1, motivation for the logarithmic measure. Separate practical, intuitive, and mathematical reasons follow the proposed representation. Parallel reason clauses make the choice assessable. Use when those reasons hold; exposition cannot replace a proof. Do not imitate period-specific vocabulary or syntax automatically.
